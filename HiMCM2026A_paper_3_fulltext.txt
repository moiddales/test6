  Floors First, Then Freedom: Ranking 25 U.S. Cities for High Schoolers
                        Who Cannot Rely on a Car
What makes a city great for a high schooler? We define the best city as the one that, for a family on a
fixed budget, clears basic floors on rent, violent crime and air quality, and then gives a public-school stu-
dent who cannot depend on car rides the most real access to education, health support, independent
travel, activities and fair treatment. The city must also keep that lead when reasonable people weigh these
things differently.
Criteria and data. We built 20 measures in seven separate domains: education, safety, health, mobility,
activities, affordability and inclusion. Every measure is computed from public data for the 25 most popu-
lous U.S. cities (Census Vintage 2025): CRDC and EDFacts school records, FBI and NHTSA FARS, EPA air
data, SAMHSA, EPA Smart Location Database, FTA NTD, HUD, BEA and HRC. We dropped nine candidate
criteria (for example weather and college rankings) and explain why (Section 3.2).
Model. (1) Three non-compensable floors screen cities before any scoring. (2) Each measure is converted
to value by a piecewise-linear curve with fixed, city-independent reference points. (3) Linear programs
test every pair of cities over the whole set of allowed preference weights. (4) Stochastic multicriteria ac-
ceptability analysis (SMAA) repeats the ranking 10,000 times under random weights and data errors. (5)
Minimax regret and ELECTRE III check a cautious decision rule and a no-weak-spot rule.
Results. Seattle is our Best City for a High Schooler.
    Under the preferences stated in our definition, Seattle ranks first in 75.0% of 10,000 simulations and in
    the top three in 99.1%.
    It has the smallest worst-case regret (0.151 on a 0–1 value scale) and passes all three floors.
    New York is second (first in 14.0% of simulations) and Denver third (7.8%), although Denver narrowly
    leads on equal domain weights (0.8210 against 0.8205).
    Among cities that pass the floors, the top five are Seattle, New York, Denver, Boston and Austin. San
    Francisco would place fourth but fails the rent floor for an $80,000 family (rent takes 50.4% of income).
Significant revisions. Auditing real data changed our model twice.
    M0→M1: a transit-frequency measure was blank for 96% of San Diego's residents, and one adult
    charter school pulled San Francisco's graduation rate to 56%. Fixing both moved San Francisco from
    13th to 8th.
    M1→M2: we replaced transit service hours with jobs reachable within 45 minutes by scheduled
    transit, so we measure what a student can actually reach.
Why it is more than our opinion. Reference points are fixed, so removing any city reverses 0 pairs of
other cities (an entropy-weight ranking reverses 106). At equal weights Denver and Seattle stay the two
leaders with data noise up to four times its size and ±10% node shifts. Under our stated preferences Seattle
stays first with node shifts (73%), with data noise quadrupled (36%), under low-substitution aggregation
(84%) and without any single preference statement (62–75%). School-age survey outcomes agree with our
domain scores: skipping school for fear of being unsafe rises with violent crime (ρ = +0.62), and sadness or
hopelessness correlates with our health domain (ρ = −0.76).
Caveat. Seattle's lead belongs to families who can afford its neighborhoods. Only 38.5% of its residents
live in census tracts that are affordable on $80,000 and well served by transit. For a $50,000 family Chicago
leads at equal weights and Columbus when cost counts double.

1. Introduction
1.1 Background
More than 15 million students attend U.S. public high schools. Most city rankings are written for
adults: they weigh job markets, nightlife and home prices. A high schooler's life runs on different
things: whether the nearest public school offers advanced courses, whether a bus can get her to
practice and back after school, whether a counselor or a clinic will see a 16-year-old, and whether
she is treated fairly. A teenager usually cannot drive and cannot choose the family budget, so the
city's value to her depends on what she can reach and afford without an adult's car.
1.2 The task and our reading of it
 1. Choose and justify criteria for a "best city for a high schooler", and discuss criteria left out.
 2. Gather and cite data, apply the model, and discuss limitations.
 3. Rank at least ten cities and reach a reasoned conclusion about the best one, under our own
    definition.
 4. Show why the ranking has merit beyond opinion, and document at least one significant revision.
 5. Write a one-to-two-page non-technical news article that includes our modeling process.
Our definition of "best". The best city is the one that, for a family on a fixed budget, clears basic floors on
cost and serious risk, and then gives a public-school student who cannot depend on car rides the most real
access to education, health support, independent travel, activities and fair treatment — and keeps that lead
across reasonable disagreements about what matters most.
Three features of this definition shape the model. Floors come before trade-offs: no number of af-
ter-school programs should make up for a violent-crime rate above 1,200 per 100,000. Real access
means measuring what a student can use (enrollments in advanced courses, jobs reachable by tran-
sit in 45 minutes), not how many buildings exist. Robustness means the answer must hold over a
whole set of reasonable weightings, not a single set we chose.
"Cannot depend on car rides." The student does not drive and has no adult who can drive her to
school, practice or work every day, so daily trips are made on foot, by bike or by transit. This de-
scribes many teenagers: in the 2022 National Household Travel Survey, half of all trips by 14–18-
year-olds were as a passenger in someone else's car, and only half of 16–17-year-olds were drivers
[26]. A city that works for such a student also works for one who sometimes gets a ride.

2. Assumptions
1. The student attends a public high school and cannot rely on daily car rides. Justification: most
   U.S. high schoolers attend public schools, and many teenagers do not drive; the problem asks
Team # TEAM_NUMBER                                                                            Page 4 of 26



   about high schoolers, not families with cars.
2. City-wide statistics describe the conditions a typical resident student meets. Justification:
   consistent neighborhood data are not available for all measures in 25 cities. We test this
   assumption with census-tract data in Section 9.3.
3. The reference family earns $80,000 a year and rents a two-bedroom home. Justification: this is
   close to the U.S. median household income; we scan $50,000–$120,000 in Section 9.2.
4. Recent multi-year averages represent current conditions. Justification: we use 2021–2025 data
   and average several years where single years are noisy (crime 2022–2025, Chicago 2021; traffic
   and air 2021–2024).
5. Once floors are met, a better result in one measure can partly make up for a worse one in
   another. Justification: this is the usual way families trade off. We test it against a non-
   compensatory method (ELECTRE III) and against low-substitution aggregation (Section 8.2).
6. Missing values are not zero. Justification: a missing report is not evidence of no service. Two of
   500 cells are missing; we show each city's rank with the missing value set to the worst and to the
   best node.

3. Criteria: What We Measure and What We Leave Out
3.1 Seven domains and twenty measures
We began from the needs of a 14–18-year-old rather than from available data. Each domain answers
one question, and the domains stay separate even when their measures are correlated. Each mea-
sure has four fixed reference points ("nodes"): the worst node is worth 0 and the best node earns the
full value of that measure. Node values come from standards where they exist (for example the 250-
students-per-counselor recommendation [16] and HUD's 30% and 50% rent-burden lines [12]) and
otherwise from round values that span realistic U.S. conditions. They were fixed before any city was
scored.

3.3 The candidate cities
The pool is the 25 most populous cities in the Census Vintage 2025 estimates [1], with each city as
the Census defines it. Indianapolis and Nashville share their governments with their counties; for
them the Census ranks the "balance", the merged city-county minus the small towns inside it that
keep their own governments (for example Belle Meade in Nashville), and we use the same area for
every measure. The list was frozen before any data were scored, so no city was added or dropped to
favor a result.

4. Data
4.1 Sources and processing
We assembled a long table of 157,256 records (121,160 numeric values) from 28 public sources. It
covers multiple years, geographic levels (school, police agency, county, metro area, transit opera-
tor) and sources [1]–[17]. From it we computed the 25 × 20 matrix used by the model. The main pro-
cessing rules:
    School measures sum all high-school-offering schools located inside each city boundary (2,264
    schools in CRDC 2023–24) and divide by their enrollment. Negative CRDC reserve codes are
    treated as missing, never as zero.
    Rates use Census city population, except where the police agency covers a larger area
    (Charlotte-Mecklenburg, Las Vegas Metropolitan and Metro Nashville), where we use the FBI
    agency population.

   Chicago's violent crime. The FBI series counts about 3,400 aggravated assaults a year in Chicago,
   against 15,606 aggravated assaults and batteries in the police department's 2021 report:
   aggravated battery is missing. We use the department's 2021 total (25,979, or 960 per 100,000) and
   keep the FBI series' relative error.
   Fares are the price of one local ride for an eligible student; Dallas and Indianapolis sell only 3-

   Located measures: traffic deaths are counted where the crash occurred and mental-health
   facilities by their postal city (no capacity data). Park access is county-scale for Charlotte and

   Area measures (air quality, rent) are weighted by the share of the city's residents in each county.
   Neighborhood measures (walkability, transit access) are population-weighted means over the
   census block groups inside each city (22,398 populated block groups).
4.2 Measurement error
Every value carries a standard deviation used in the uncertainty analysis:
   Multi-year measures (crime, traffic, air): the standard error of the annual mean.
   School measures: the difference between the two CRDC waves (2021–22 and 2023–24) divided by
   √2, the sample standard deviation of two values.
   Graduation rates published as ranges: the range width converted to a 90% interval. Chicago and
   Seattle report no school-level rates for this cohort, so we use their official district rates (Illinois
   Report Card, Washington OSPI) and treat them as exact. Other cities use cohort-weighted school
   rates.
   Counts (youth mental-health facilities): the Poisson standard deviation √n.
Seven measures are published standards or modeled values and are treated as exact: rent (HUD Fair
Market Rents), price parity, youth fare, teen wage, HRC score, walkability and transit access. Errors
are independent across cities and measures; Section 8.3 tests errors shared within a domain.
4.3 Missing data
Two of the 500 cells are missing:
   San Francisco reports no school athletics in schools enrolling 94% of its students, which we treat

5.2 Step 1: floors that cannot be bought back
Some conditions are needs, not preferences. A city fails if any of three raw values crosses a floor:

The rent floor is HUD's "severe burden" line [12]. The violent-crime floor is about three times the
2023 national rate of 363.8 per 100,000 [5]. The air floor means that air is unhealthy for students with
asthma on nearly one day in three. No city's value crosses the last two floors, and three cities come
within measurement error. Traffic deaths stay in the safety domain but are not a floor: between our
cities the yearly death rate differs by at most 15 per 100,000 (2.8 in New York, 18.3 in Phoenix), too
small a risk to override every other strength. A floor is checked on the raw value, before any weight-
ing, so no other strength can offset it.
Because each value has an error, a city has three states for each floor. It passes if its 90% interval
            lies fully on the safe side, fails if it lies fully on the wrong side, and is pending otherwise.
The joint chance of passing all three floors is estimated as the share of 20,000 simulated data sets in
which all three values pass. Floors were also scanned over ranges (Section 9.2), because no floor
value is universal.

8. Why the Ranking Is More Than Our Opinion
8.1 Fixed reference points make the ranking stable when the pool changes
Popular objective methods such as entropy weighting derive weights from how much the measures
vary among the candidates. Their results therefore depend on who else is in the pool. We tested this
directly: removing one city at a time and re-scoring the other 24 reverses 106 pairs in total over the
25 removals under min-max entropy weights. Under our fixed reference points it reverses 0, be-
cause a city's score depends only on its own data. The final ranking in Table 1, by mean SMAA rank,
reverses only 2 pairs over the 25 removals, both between cities we already treat as tied (San Diego
and Chicago; Houston and Oklahoma City). Entropy weighting would also put the largest weight on
transit access (0.134) simply because New York is an outlier, and it would crown New York.

10. Strengths and Weaknesses
Strengths
   Floors before trade-offs. No strength can compensate for unaffordable rent or high risk, and
   borderline cities are flagged as pending instead of being passed or failed silently.
   Exact robustness, not just one weighting. Linear programs test every pair over the whole
   preference set. SMAA turns that into frequencies with a standard error of at most 0.005, and
   minimax regret serves cautious families.
   Stable when the pool changes. Fixed reference points mean that adding or removing a city
   reverses no other pair of reference scores.
   Data-driven revisions. Two revisions came from errors found in real records, with controlled
   comparisons.
   Reproducible. Every number in this paper comes from archived public data and our scripts.
Weaknesses and what would fix them
   Proxy for destinations. The accessibility measure counts jobs, not schools, parks or teen
   programs. Point-level routing to actual facilities with current timetables would measure student
   trips directly.
   Inclusion is measured by policy. HRC points describe what a city promises; school-level climate
   surveys for all 25 cities would measure students' experience.
   School location is not school access. We use schools located inside each city; attendance-zone
   and lottery rules would show which schools a student can actually attend.
   Some measures are dated. Graduation rates are for the 2020–21 cohort and accessibility uses
   2017 jobs with 2020 schedules. Seattle's lead depends on graduation: removing it leaves Seattle,
   Denver and New York tied under (Section 8.3).
   Crime definitions differ by agency. Chicago's FBI series omits aggravated battery, so we used the
   police department's 2021 total; with the FBI series Chicago would rank 3rd (Section 8.3). A
   common incident-level definition for all 25 cities would remove this judgment.
   Stated preferences are ours. P1–P6 follow our definition. A family with other priorities can use
   the profile results or change the statements.

11. Conclusion
 1. Criteria. We measured 20 conditions in seven separate domains that matter to a high schooler
    who cannot rely on a car, and left out nine criteria with reasons.
 2. Ranking. We ranked all 25 of the largest U.S. cities. Under our definition, Seattle is the Best City
    for a High Schooler: first in 75.0% of 10,000 simulations, top three in 99.1%, smallest worst-case
    regret (0.151). It passes every floor for an $80,000 family.
 3. Close contenders. New York is second, with by far the most transit access but high rents. Denver
    is a near-tie on equal weights (0.8210 vs 0.8205) and leads when affordability or health matter
    more. At $50,000 Chicago leads at equal weights and Columbus when cost counts double.
 4. Merit beyond opinion. Under our stated preferences Seattle stays first with larger data errors,
    node shifts, low-substitution aggregation, any one preference statement removed, and any one
    measure removed except advanced courses. Three of four independent youth outcomes agree
    with our measures.
 5. Revisions. Two revisions from real data replaced a broken transit field, removed adult-school
    distortions from graduation rates, and moved the model from buses supplied to places
    reachable.
Seattle's lead is clear under our stated preferences but narrow at equal weights, and it belongs
mostly to families who can afford its connected neighborhoods. The next step is to measure what
students can reach from where they can afford to live.

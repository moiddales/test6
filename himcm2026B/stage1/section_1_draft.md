# 1. Cooling service and system boundaries

An open freezer must maintain acceptable product temperatures while permitting access. Compressor COP alone does not measure that service. Warm-air entrainment brings sensible heat and moisture; the latter adds condensation, frost and defrost loads. Radiation through the opening, conduction, lights, fans and heaters also matter. Night covers and doors can reduce opening loads, but access, visibility, condensation control and maintenance remain competing objectives.

We define a service ratio as compliant display-area-hours divided by energy at a stated boundary. The cabinet-only denominator includes compressor and auxiliary electricity. A whole-store denominator also includes the incremental HVAC effect: extracting heat from a store may reduce cooling demand or increase heating demand. Energy savings therefore depend on climate and equipment placement. Product-temperature compliance is a prerequisite, not a result of our illustrative load calculation.

The optional numerical example in `freezer.py` assumes a low-temperature cabinet and an infiltration share. It is not calibrated by the medium-temperature case study of Sarhadian et al. (2004). That paper is useful evidence that infiltration matters; it cannot establish our freezer's exact infiltration, frost production or door savings. A regulatory energy limit likewise cannot validate a prediction. The code's accounting identities check implementation only.

For public spaces we transfer this boundary discipline: the useful service is relief experienced by people over time, rather than all sensible cooling produced at the outlet. We report electricity, water, residual heat exposure and cost alongside the service ratio. Neither an attractive ratio nor a modeled temperature guarantees safe conditions for every person.
